01. Fixed Income Essentials & Bond Pricing Mechanics

A bond represents a contractual debt obligation between the issuer (borrower) and the investor (creditor). The fundamental value of any default-free fixed-income instrument equals the present value of its future cash flows discounted at the market discount rate (YTM).

The Universal Bond Valuation Equation

Fundamental Bond Pricing Formula
$$PV = \sum_{t=1}^N \frac{PMT}{(1 + r)^t} + \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^N}$$ $$\text{Where: } PMT = \text{Coupon payment per period}, \quad FV = \text{Face (Par) value}, \quad r = \text{Market discount rate per period}$$

Full Price (Dirty Price) vs Flat Price (Clean Price)

In secondary bond markets, transactions rarely settle precisely on a coupon payment date. The buyer must compensate the seller for accrued interest earned from the last coupon date to settlement date:

Clean vs Dirty Bond Pricing & Accrued Interest
$$PV^{\text{Full}} = PV^{\text{Flat}} + AI$$ $$AI = PMT \times \left( \frac{t}{T} \right)$$ $$\text{Where: } t = \text{Days accrued since prior coupon}, \quad T = \text{Total days in coupon period}$$
  • Actual/Actual Convention: Standard for government bonds. Exact calendar days elapsed divided by actual days in coupon period.
  • 30/360 Convention: Standard for corporate and municipal bonds. Assumes 30 days per month and 360 days per year.

Matrix Pricing for Illiquid & Unrated Bonds

When a target bond is illiquid or newly issued without market quotes, analysts utilize Matrix Pricing (linear interpolation of yields on comparable liquid benchmark bonds matching maturity and credit quality) to establish the appropriate discounting yield:

Matrix Pricing Linear Interpolation
$$y_{\text{target}} = y_{\text{short}} + \left( \frac{T_{\text{target}} - T_{\text{short}}}{T_{\text{long}} - T_{\text{short}}} \right) \times (y_{\text{long}} - y_{\text{short}})$$

Worked Numerical Example 1: Clean vs. Dirty Price & Matrix Pricing

Part A (Accrued Interest & Full Price): A 6.0% semiannual-pay Treasury bond (Par = 1,000 USD) settles 62 days into a 182-day coupon period (Actual/Actual convention) at a quoted flat (clean) price of 1,018.50 USD.

  • Semiannual coupon payment: $PMT = \frac{6.0\% \times 1,000}{2} = 30.00\text{ USD}$
  • Accrued Interest: $AI = 30.00 \times \frac{62}{182} = 10.22\text{ USD}$
  • Full (Dirty) Price paid by buyer: $PV^{\text{Full}} = 1,018.50 + 10.22 = 1,028.72\text{ USD}$

Part B (Matrix Pricing): A newly issued 4.2-year BBB-rated corporate bond lacks market quotes. Comparable liquid BBB bonds yield 5.20% (3.0-year maturity) and 5.80% (5.0-year maturity):

$$y_{\text{4.2Y}} = 5.20\% + \left(\frac{4.2 - 3.0}{5.0 - 3.0}\right) \times (5.80\% - 5.20\%) = 5.20\% + 0.60 \times 0.60\% = 5.56\%$$

Floating-Rate Notes (FRNs), Repo & Shorting Fixed Income (CBOK VII)

Floating-Rate Notes (FRNs) reset coupons periodically relative to a market reference rate (MRR / SOFR) plus a fixed Quoted Margin ($QM$). When credit quality changes after issuance, the market discounts cash flows using MRR plus the required Discount Margin ($DM$) ($PV < \text{Par}$ if $DM > QM$).

Repurchase Agreements (Repo) & Shorting Bonds: A repo is a collateralized short-term loan where a dealer sells a bond today and agrees to repurchase it at a higher price on a future date. Conversely, investors short fixed-income securities by entering a reverse repo (lending cash to borrow the bond collateral), selling the bond in the market, and obligating themselves to reimburse the lender for any interim coupon payments. High demand to short a specific Treasury issue drives its collateral rate down—trading "on special" (below the general collateral repo rate).

Repurchase Agreement (Repo) Pricing & Haircut
$$\text{Initial Loan (Purchase Price)} = \text{Collateral Market Value} \times (1 - \text{Repo Margin / Haircut})$$ $$\text{Repurchase Price} = \text{Purchase Price} \times \left(1 + \text{Repo Rate} \times \frac{\text{Days}}{360}\right)$$

Worked Numerical Example 2: Repo Haircut, Dollar Interest & Short Financing

Scenario: A dealer finances 10,000,000 USD market value of corporate bonds via a 30-day repo agreement with a 4.0% haircut and a 4.80% annualized repo rate (Actual/360 convention).

  1. Purchase Price (Cash Borrowed): $10,000,000 \times (1 - 0.04) = 9,600,000\text{ USD}$
  2. Repo Dollar Interest: $9,600,000 \times 0.0480 \times \frac{30}{360} = 38,400\text{ USD}$
  3. Repurchase Price at Maturity: $9,600,000 + 38,400 = 9,638,400\text{ USD}$

Short-Selling Link: A hedge fund acting as the reverse-repo counterparty lends 9,600,000 USD, receives the bonds as collateral, shorts them for cash, and earns 4.80% on its posted cash margin (rebate) while paying the bond's accrued coupon to the collateral owner.

02. Term Structure of Interest Rates & Yield Curves

The term structure of interest rates describes the relationship between maturity tenors and yields for benchmark bonds of identical creditworthiness. A single discount rate (YTM) for all cash flows is a simplification; rigorous valuation requires discounting each cash flow at its specific spot rate.

Spot Rates & Curve Bootstrapping

The Spot Rate ($S_t$) is the yield to maturity on a zero-coupon bond maturing at period $t$. Bootstrapping systematically extracts zero-coupon spot rates from par yield curves:

Spot Rate Valuation & Bootstrapping Equation
$$PV = \frac{PMT}{1 + S_1} + \frac{PMT}{(1 + S_2)^2} + \cdots + \frac{PMT + FV}{(1 + S_N)^N}$$ $$1 = \frac{c}{1 + S_1} + \frac{c}{(1 + S_2)^2} + \frac{1 + c}{(1 + S_3)^3} \implies S_3 = \left( \frac{1 + c}{1 - \frac{c}{1+S_1} - \frac{c}{(1+S_2)^2}} \right)^{1/3} - 1$$

Forward Rates & Forward Rate Parity

A forward rate $f(A, B-A)$ is an interest rate agreed upon today for a loan beginning at future period $A$ and maturing at period $B$. Under no-arbitrage conditions:

Forward Rate Parity Identity
$$(1 + S_B)^B = (1 + S_A)^A \times [1 + f(A, B-A)]^{B-A}$$ $$[1 + f(1, 1)] = \frac{(1 + S_2)^2}{(1 + S_1)^1}$$
Visual 1: Benchmark Spot Curve vs Implied Forward Curve
When the spot yield curve is upward sloping, the forward curve always lies above the spot curve ($f(t-1, 1) > S_t > y_{par}$).

Term Structure Theories

Hypothesis Core Principle Yield Curve Interpretation
Pure Expectations Theory Forward rates are unbiased predictors of future spot rates. No risk premium required for longer maturities. Upward slope indicates market expects future short-term interest rates to rise.
Liquidity Preference Theory Investors demand a liquidity premium ($L_t$) for tying up funds in longer-term, less liquid bonds. Curves slope upward naturally even if expected future spot rates are flat or slightly declining.
Segmented Markets Theory Yields at each maturity are determined independently by supply and demand of institutional habitat participants. Shape is determined by supply-demand imbalances across market segments (e.g. pension funds in 30Y).
Preferred Habitat Theory Market participants have preferred maturities but will cross segments if yield premium is sufficiently attractive. Humps and twists reflect market-clearing risk premiums required to induce cross-segment capital migration.

Worked Numerical Example 3: Bootstrapping Spot Rates & Forward Rate Parity

Scenario: Given annual-pay benchmark government par yields of $y_1 = 4.00\%$, $y_2 = 4.50\%$, and $y_3 = 5.00\%$, bootstrap the spot curve ($S_1, S_2, S_3$) and derive the 2-year forward rate starting in 1 year, $f(1,2)$.

  1. 1-Year Spot Rate ($S_1$): $S_1 = y_1 = 4.0000\%$
  2. 2-Year Spot Rate ($S_2$): Using a 2-year par bond ($c = 4.50\%$): $$1.00 = \frac{0.045}{1.04} + \frac{1.045}{(1+S_2)^2} \implies (1+S_2)^2 = \frac{1.045}{1 - 0.043269} = 1.09226 \implies S_2 = 4.5113\%$$
  3. 3-Year Spot Rate ($S_3$): Using a 3-year par bond ($c = 5.00\%$): $$1.00 = \frac{0.05}{1.04} + \frac{0.05}{(1.045113)^2} + \frac{1.05}{(1+S_3)^3} \implies S_3 = \left(\frac{1.05}{1 - 0.048077 - 0.045777}\right)^{1/3} - 1 = 5.0341\%$$
  4. Implied 2-Year Forward Rate 1 Year Forward ($f(1,2)$): $$[1 + f(1,2)]^2 = \frac{(1+S_3)^3}{(1+S_1)^1} = \frac{(1.050341)^3}{1.0400} = 1.11408 \implies f(1,2) = \sqrt{1.11408} - 1 = 5.5550\%$$

Check: Because the spot curve is upward sloping, $f(1,2) = 5.56\% > S_3 = 5.03\% > y_3 = 5.00\%$.

Equilibrium vs. Arbitrage-Free Term Structure Models (L2) & Yield Curve Strategies

Model / Class Drift & Volatility Specification Key Properties & Exam Distinction
Vasicek Model (Equilibrium) $dr = a(b - r)dt + \sigma dW$ Mean-reverting ($b$ = long-run mean); constant volatility $\sigma$; permits negative interest rates.
Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) (Equilibrium) $dr = a(b - r)dt + \sigma\sqrt{r}\,dW$ Mean-reverting; volatility proportional to $\sqrt{r}$ (rises with rate level); prevents negative rates if $2ab \ge \sigma^2$.
Ho-Lee Model (Arbitrage-Free) $dr = \theta_t\,dt + \sigma dW$ Calibrated exactly to today's observed spot curve via time-dependent drift $\theta_t$; normal distribution (allows negative rates).
Kalotay-Williams-Fabozzi (KWF) (Arbitrage-Free) $d\ln(r) = \theta_t\,dt + \sigma dW$ Lognormal short-rate distribution; calibrated to market term structure; guarantees strictly positive rates with constant proportional vol.

Active Curve Strategies: Under an upward-sloping, stable yield curve, Riding the Yield Curve purchases bonds with maturities longer than the holding horizon so the bond "rolls down" the curve to lower yields and appreciates in price. The MRR–OIS Spread (difference between interbank funding rates and overnight index swap rates) serves as a primary gauge of banking-sector funding stress.

03. Yield Measures, Benchmark Spreads & Option-Adjusted Spreads

Credit risk, liquidity risk, and taxation cause corporate bonds to trade at a spread above default-free benchmark government bonds.

Key Spread Definitions

  • G-Spread (Government Spread): Yield spread between a corporate bond and an interpolated government benchmark bond of identical maturity.
  • I-Spread (Interpolated Spread): Yield spread over standard interbank swap benchmark rates (MRR / SOFR swap rates).
  • Zero-Volatility Spread (Z-Spread): Constant basis point spread added to each spot rate on the benchmark zero curve to equate the present value of bond cash flows to its market price: $$PV = \sum_{t=1}^N \frac{CF_t}{(1 + S_t + Z)^t}$$
  • Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS): The spread isolated after stripping out the cost of embedded options (calls, puts). Measures pure credit and liquidity risk: $$\text{Option Cost (bps)} = Z\text{-spread} - OAS$$ $$\text{For a Callable Bond: } Z\text{-spread} > OAS \implies \text{Option Cost} > 0$$ $$\text{For a Putable Bond: } Z\text{-spread} < OAS \implies \text{Option Cost} < 0$$
  • Current Yield, Yield-to-Call (YTC) & Yield-to-Worst (YTW): Current yield $= \frac{\text{Annual Coupon}}{\text{Flat Price}}$. For callable bonds, compute YTM and YTC to every call schedule date; Yield-to-Worst (YTW) is the minimum of YTM and all YTCs.

Worked Numerical Example 4: Yield-to-Worst (YTW), Z-Spread & OAS Decomposition

Scenario: A 10-year, 6.0% semiannual coupon corporate bond (Par = 1,000 USD) trades at 1,060.00 USD and is callable in 4 years at 1,030.00 USD. A comparable government benchmark yields 4.00%, the bond's $Z$-spread is 148 bps, and the embedded call option cost is estimated at 36 bps.

  1. Yield-to-Maturity (YTM): Solving $1,060 = \sum_{t=1}^{20} \frac{30}{(1+r)^{t}} + \frac{1,000}{(1+r)^{20}}$ yields semiannual $r = 2.61\% \implies \text{YTM} = 5.22\%$.
  2. Yield-to-Call (YTC at 4Y): Solving $1,060 = \sum_{t=1}^{8} \frac{30}{(1+r_c)^{t}} + \frac{1,030}{(1+r_c)^{8}}$ yields semiannual $r_c = 2.505\% \implies \text{YTC} = 5.01\%$.
  3. Yield-to-Worst (YTW): $\min(5.22\%, 5.01\%) = 5.01\%$ (YTC governs because bond trades above call price).
  4. G-Spread & OAS: $\text{G-Spread} = 5.22\% - 4.00\% = 122\text{ bps}$. Removing the call option cost gives pure credit/liquidity compensation: $OAS = Z\text{-spread} - \text{Option Cost} = 148 - 36 = 112\text{ bps}$.

Sustainability Considerations in Fixed Income (CBOK VII)

  • Green, Social & Sustainability Bonds: Use-of-proceeds debt ring-fenced to environmental or social projects; credit risk remains backed by the issuer's general balance sheet. When investor demand outpaces supply, green bonds may price at a slight yield discount (Greenium).
  • Sustainability-Linked Bonds (SLBs): General corporate purpose debt whose financial terms (typically a coupon step-up of +25 bps to +50 bps) adjust if the issuer fails to achieve predefined Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs) by a target observation date.

04. Interest Rate Risk: Duration, Convexity & Sensitivity

Duration measures a bond's price sensitivity to changes in benchmark interest rates. Convexity measures the rate of change of duration, providing a second-order curvature adjustment for large interest rate shocks.

Macaulay, Modified & Effective Duration

Duration Formulas & Relationships
$$MacDur = \sum_{t=1}^N \left[ t \times \frac{CF_t / (1 + y)^t}{PV} \right]$$ $$ModDur = \frac{MacDur}{1 + y / k} \quad (k = \text{compounding frequency per year})$$ $$EffDur = \frac{PV_- - PV_+}{2 \times \Delta y \times PV_0} \quad (\text{Mandatory for bonds with embedded options})$$

Convexity & The Combined Price Approximation

Because the price-yield relationship of a straight bond is convex (curves upward), a linear duration estimate underestimates price increases when yields fall and overestimates price declines when yields rise:

Full Taylor-Series Price Change Approximation
$$\% \Delta PV \approx -ModDur \times \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \times Convexity \times (\Delta y)^2$$ $$ApproxConvexity = \frac{PV_- + PV_+ - 2PV_0}{(\Delta y)^2 \times PV_0}$$
Visual 2: Price-Yield Convexity vs Linear Duration Approximation
The tangible benefit of positive convexity: the bond price rises more when yields drop than it falls when yields increase by the same magnitude.

Key Rate Duration (KRD), Money Duration & Empirical Duration

Key rate duration measures bond price sensitivity to a 100 bps shift at a single maturity point on the spot curve while holding all other points constant. The sum of all key rate durations equals the effective duration for a parallel shift:

$$EffDur = \sum_{i=1}^M KRD_i, \qquad \text{MoneyDur} = ModDur \times PV^{\text{Full}}, \qquad \text{PVBP} = \frac{PV_- - PV_+}{2} \text{ (for } \Delta y = 1\text{ bp)}$$

Portfolio Duration equals the market-value weighted average of constituent bond durations ($Dur_P = \sum w_i Dur_i$), NOT an equally weighted average. Analytical vs. Empirical Duration: Analytical formulas assume benchmark yields and credit spreads move independently. In "flight-to-safety" recessions, government benchmark yields fall while high-yield credit spreads widen simultaneously—making Empirical Duration (estimated via historical regression of bond prices on benchmark yields) substantially lower than analytical duration for high-yield corporates.

Worked Numerical Example 5: Effective Duration, Convexity & Key Rate Duration Twist

Scenario: A bond trades at $PV_0 = 100.00\text{ USD}$. Shifting the benchmark par curve by $\Delta y = \pm 25\text{ bps}$ ($0.0025$) produces $PV_- = 101.84\text{ USD}$ and $PV_+ = 98.24\text{ USD}$. Its key rate profile is $KRD_2 = 1.20$, $KRD_5 = 2.80$, $KRD_{10} = 3.20$.

  1. Effective Duration: $EffDur = \frac{101.84 - 98.24}{2 \times 0.0025 \times 100.00} = \frac{3.60}{0.50} = 7.20$ (Notice $1.20 + 2.80 + 3.20 = 7.20$).
  2. Effective Convexity: $EffConv = \frac{101.84 + 98.24 - 2(100.00)}{(0.0025)^2 \times 100.00} = \frac{0.08}{0.000625} = 128.00$.
  3. +100 bps Parallel Yield Increase ($\Delta y = +0.01$): $$\%\Delta PV \approx -7.20(0.01) + \frac{1}{2}(128.00)(0.01)^2 = -7.20\% + 0.64\% = -6.56\%$$
  4. Non-Parallel Curve Flattening Twist ($\Delta y_2 = +40\text{ bps}$, $\Delta y_5 = +10\text{ bps}$, $\Delta y_{10} = -25\text{ bps}$): $$\%\Delta PV \approx -(1.20 \times 0.40\% + 2.80 \times 0.10\% + 3.20 \times (-0.25\%)) = -(0.48\% + 0.28\% - 0.80\%) = +0.04\%$$

05. Bonds with Embedded Options & Binomial Trees

Bonds containing call, put, or conversion features cannot be valued with standard yield-to-maturity discounting due to cash flow uncertainty contingent on path-dependent interest rate outcomes.

Callable vs Putable Bond Valuation

Embedded Option Decomposition
$$V_{\text{Callable Bond}} = V_{\text{Straight Bond}} - V_{\text{Call Option}}$$ $$V_{\text{Putable Bond}} = V_{\text{Straight Bond}} + V_{\text{Put Option}}$$
Negative Convexity Trap: As benchmark yields decline, the probability of the issuer calling the bond increases dramatically. The price of a callable bond compression ceiling is reached at the call price. At low yields, callable bonds exhibit negative convexity (falling yields yield diminishing price gains).

Binomial Interest Rate Tree Backward Induction

A binomial tree models interest rate evolution under lognormal volatility: $r_{1, u} = r_{1, d} e^{2\sigma}$. Valuation proceeds backwards from maturity ($T$) to node 0:

Backward Induction Valuation Node Equation
$$V_{\text{node}} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{V_{u} + PMT}{1 + r_{\text{node}}} + \frac{V_{d} + PMT}{1 + r_{\text{node}}} \right]$$ $$\text{Callable Node Decision: } V = \min(\text{Calculated Node Value}, \text{Call Price})$$ $$\text{Putable Node Decision: } V = \max(\text{Calculated Node Value}, \text{Put Price})$$

Worked Numerical Example 6: 2-Period Binomial Tree Backward Induction (Callable Bond)

Scenario: Value a 2-year, 6.0% annual-pay bond (Par = 100.00) callable at 101.00 at $t=1$. The calibrated 1-year forward rates are $r_0 = 4.00\%$ at $t=0$, and $r_{1,u} = 5.50\%$, $r_{1,d} = 4.20\%$ at $t=1$.

  1. Straight Bond at $t=1$ ($CF_2 = 106.00$): $$V_{1,u}^{\text{straight}} = \frac{106.00}{1.055} = 100.4739, \qquad V_{1,d}^{\text{straight}} = \frac{106.00}{1.042} = 101.7274$$ $$V_0^{\text{straight}} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{100.4739 + 6}{1.04} + \frac{101.7274 + 6}{1.04}\right] = 102.9814$$
  2. Callable Bond Decision at $t=1$ ($V_{\text{call}} = \min(V_{\text{calc}}, 101.00)$):
    • Upper node: $\min(100.4739, 101.00) = 100.4739$ (not called).
    • Lower node: $\min(101.7274, 101.00) = 101.0000$ (called at 101.00!).
  3. Callable Value & Embedded Call Option Value at $t=0$: $$V_0^{\text{callable}} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{100.4739 + 6}{1.04} + \frac{101.0000 + 6}{1.04}\right] = 102.6317$$ $$V_{\text{Call Option}} = V_0^{\text{straight}} - V_0^{\text{callable}} = 102.9814 - 102.6317 = 0.3497$$

Convertible Bonds Valuation & Parity Metrics (L2)

A Convertible Bond grants the holder the right to convert the bond into a specified number of common shares (Conversion Ratio). Its value cannot fall below the greater of its straight debt value or conversion value:

Convertible Bond Identities
$$\text{Conversion Value} = \text{Conversion Ratio} \times \text{Stock Price}, \qquad \text{Minimum Value} = \max(\text{Straight Value}, \text{Conversion Value})$$ $$\text{Market Conversion Price} = \frac{\text{Convertible Bond Price}}{\text{Conversion Ratio}}, \qquad \text{Conv. Premium Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Conv. Price} - \text{Stock Price}}{\text{Stock Price}}$$

Worked Numerical Example 7: Convertible Bond Parity & Premiums

Scenario: A convertible bond (Par = 1,000 USD) has a Conversion Ratio of 25 shares, Straight Value of 940 USD, and trades at 1,144 USD when the underlying common stock trades at 44.00 USD.

  • Conversion Value: $25 \times 44.00 = 1,100.00\text{ USD}$
  • Minimum Value: $\max(940.00, 1,100.00) = 1,100.00\text{ USD}$
  • Market Conversion Price: $\frac{1,144}{25} = 45.76\text{ USD}$ (effective entry price per share via the bond).
  • Market Conversion Premium Ratio: $\frac{45.76 - 44.00}{44.00} = 4.00\%$ (or $\frac{1,144 - 1,100}{1,100} = 4.00\%$).
  • Premium Over Straight Value (Downside Risk): $\frac{1,144 - 940}{940} = 21.70\%$.

06. Credit Analysis, Expected Loss & Structural Models

Credit analysis evaluates an issuer's default risk and creditworthiness. Credit risk comprises two core components: default probability and recovery rate upon default.

Expected Loss Mathematics

Expected Loss & Recovery Rate
$$\text{Expected Loss} = \text{Probability of Default (PD)} \times \text{Loss Given Default (LGD)}$$ $$\text{LGD} = 1 - \text{Recovery Rate (RR)}$$ $$\text{Present Value of Expected Loss (CVA)} = PV_{\text{Default-Free}} - PV_{\text{Risky}}$$

Structural vs Reduced Form Credit Models

Attribute Structural Models (Merton 1974) Reduced Form Models
Core Foundation Company's balance sheet structure: equity is a European call option on firm assets ($V_A$) with strike equal to debt face value ($K$). Statistical intensity process where default occurs as an exogenous Poisson jump process.
Default Condition Default occurs at maturity $T$ if total asset value falls below debt obligations: $V_A(T) < K$. Default can occur randomly at any instant prior to maturity based on macro hazard rates.
Inputs Required Market value of firm assets, asset return volatility ($\sigma_A$), balance sheet debt levels. Market credit spreads, macroeconomic covariates, historical rating transition matrices.

4 Cs of Credit, Sovereign/Municipal/HY Debt & CDS Mechanics (L1 & L2)

  • The 4 Cs of Corporate Credit: Capacity (EBITDA coverage, FCF/Debt), Collateral (asset quality, seniority waterfall), Covenants (affirmative & negative restrictions), and Character (management integrity & capital allocation history).
  • Sovereign, Municipal & High-Yield Debt: Sovereigns lack collateral and rely on fiscal/monetary institutional strength and FX reserves. US Municipal bonds divide into General Obligation (GO) (tax-backed) vs. Revenue Bonds (project-backed; higher risk); evaluate via Tax-Equivalent Yield $= \frac{y_{\text{muni}}}{1 - t}$. High-yield issuers emphasize structural subordination, liquidity runways, and recovery waterfalls.
  • Credit Default Swaps (CDS) & Upfront Payments: Protection buyer pays standardized fixed coupon (100 bps IG / 500 bps HY) to protection seller. Upfront payment reconciles coupon to market spread: $$\text{Upfront Payment (\%)} \approx (\text{CDS Spread} - \text{Fixed Coupon}) \times \text{EffDur}_{\text{CDS}}$$ $$\Delta\text{Value}_{\text{Buyer}} \approx \Delta\text{Spread} \times \text{EffDur}_{\text{CDS}} \times \text{Notional}, \qquad POD(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$$ Basis & Curve Trades: Negative basis trade (buy bond + buy CDS when CDS spread $<$ bond credit spread). Credit curve steepener (sell short-dated CDS protection + buy long-dated CDS protection).

Worked Numerical Example 8: Multi-Period Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA)

Scenario: Value a 2-year, 5.0% annual-pay corporate bond (Par = 1,000 USD) given a flat 3.00% risk-free rate, conditional annual hazard rate $PD = 2.00\%$, and Recovery Rate $RR = 40\%$ ($LGD = 60\%$).

  1. Default-Free Bond Value: $V_{\text{ND}} = \frac{50}{1.03} + \frac{1,050}{1.03^2} = 48.54 + 989.73 = 1,038.27\text{ USD}$.
  2. Year 1 Expected Loss: Exposure $= 50 + \frac{1,050}{1.03} = 1,069.42\text{ USD}$. $EL_1 = 0.02 \times 0.60 \times 1,069.42 = 12.83\text{ USD}$. $PV(EL_1) = \frac{12.83}{1.03} = 12.46\text{ USD}$.
  3. Year 2 Expected Loss: Unconditional $PD_2 = (1 - 0.02) \times 0.02 = 1.96\%$. Exposure $= 1,050\text{ USD}$. $EL_2 = 0.0196 \times 0.60 \times 1,050 = 12.35\text{ USD}$. $PV(EL_2) = \frac{12.35}{1.03^2} = 11.64\text{ USD}$.
  4. CVA & Fair Risky Value: $CVA = 12.46 + 11.64 = 24.10\text{ USD} \implies V_{\text{Risky}} = 1,038.27 - 24.10 = 1,014.17\text{ USD}$.

Worked Numerical Example 9: CDS Upfront Payment & Mark-to-Market Valuation

Scenario: An investor buys 10,000,000 USD notional of 5-year high-yield CDS protection (standardized fixed coupon = 500 bps) when the market CDS spread is 620 bps and $EffDur_{\text{CDS}} = 4.2$.

  1. Initial Upfront Payment: $(6.20\% - 5.00\%) \times 4.2 = 1.20\% \times 4.2 = 5.04\%$ of notional ($504,000\text{ USD}$ paid by buyer to seller at initiation).
  2. Mark-to-Market After Spread Widening: Suppose credit fundamentals deteriorate and the CDS spread widens by $+80\text{ bps}$ to 700 bps (new duration $= 4.0$): $$\text{Buyer MTM Gain} \approx +0.0080 \times 4.0 \times 10,000,000\text{ USD} = +320,000\text{ USD}$$

07. Asset-Backed (ABS) & Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)

Securitization pools illiquid financial assets (residential mortgages, auto loans, credit card debt) and converts them into tradable marketable securities through a bankruptcy-remote Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV).

Prepayment Risk: Contraction vs Extension Risk

  • Contraction Risk: Occurs when interest rates decline significantly. Homeowners refinance mortgages at lower rates, accelerating principal prepayments. MBS investors receive early principal and are forced to reinvest at lower prevailing rates, shortening maturity.
  • Extension Risk: Occurs when interest rates rise. Homeowners delay moving or refinancing, slowing prepayments. Investors remain locked into below-market coupon payments, extending average life.

Prepayment Metrics: SMM & CPR

Prepayment Rate Identities
$$SMM = \frac{\text{Prepayment in Month } t}{\text{Beginning Mortgage Balance} - \text{Scheduled Principal}}$$ $$CPR = 1 - (1 - SMM)^{12} \iff SMM = 1 - (1 - CPR)^{1/12}$$

Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO), CMBS, CDO & Covered Bonds (CBOK VII)

  • Sequential-Pay Tranches: Principal payments retire tranches in sequential order (Tranche A first, then B, then C). Early tranches absorb contraction risk; later tranches absorb extension risk.
  • PAC & Support Tranches: Planned Amortization Class (PAC) tranches provide stable cash flows within a specified prepayment collar. The support tranche absorbs prepayments above or below the band, exhibiting high volatility.
  • Commercial MBS (CMBS) & Credit Enhancement: Non-recourse loans analyzed via DSCR ($\text{NOI} / \text{Debt Service}$) and LTV. Call protection is embedded via lockout, defeasance, and yield maintenance. ABS/CDO waterfalls combine internal credit enhancement (overcollateralization, subordination, excess spread) to shield senior tranches.
  • Covered Bonds: On-balance-sheet senior debt backed by dual recourse—both to the issuing financial institution and to a ring-fenced, dynamic cover pool (non-performing loans must be replaced). Redemption structures include hard-bullet, soft-bullet, and conditional pass-through.

Worked Numerical Example 10: MBS Prepayment Speed (PSA, CPR & SMM)

Scenario: A residential mortgage pass-through pool has a beginning balance of 50,000,000 USD in Month 20, scheduled principal payment of 180,000 USD, and prepays at 150% PSA.

  1. 100% PSA Benchmark CPR (Month 20): Ramps linearly at 0.20% per month up to Month 30 ($6.0\%$): $\text{CPR}_{100\%}(20) = 6.0\% \times \frac{20}{30} = 4.00\%$.
  2. 150% PSA CPR: $\text{CPR}_{150\%} = 1.50 \times 4.00\% = 6.00\%$.
  3. Single Monthly Mortality (SMM): $SMM = 1 - (1 - 0.060)^{1/12} = 0.005143 \; (0.5143\%)$.
  4. Monthly Principal Prepayment: $0.005143 \times (50,000,000 - 180,000) = 256,225\text{ USD}$.

Worked Numerical Example 11: ABS / CDO Subordination Waterfall

Scenario: A 100,000,000 USD collateralized debt structure issues Senior Tranche (75,000,000 USD), Mezzanine Tranche (15,000,000 USD), and Equity/First-Loss Tranche (10,000,000 USD). Cumulative net default losses reach 18,000,000 USD.

  • Equity Tranche: Absorbs the first 10,000,000 USD of losses $\implies$ completely wiped out (0 USD remaining).
  • Mezzanine Tranche: Absorbs remaining $18,000,000 - 10,000,000 = 8,000,000\text{ USD}$ $\implies$ impaired to 7,000,000 USD principal.
  • Senior Tranche: Fully protected by 25% subordination buffer $\implies$ 75,000,000 USD principal remains 100% intact.
